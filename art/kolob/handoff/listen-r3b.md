# Listening packet: round 3b

*For the owner. Round 3b built the meeting out "as though there were no
technical constraints", as you asked: the whole ward, the organist, every
style and guest, and now the shape of a visit. Nothing has been published,
and VERSION is unchanged. This page says what is new to hear, how big each
thing is, where to hear it, and what it costs, so you can decide what to
scale back.*

*Revised after the critic's review (round 2 of step 4). The corrections
are folded in below: the reckoning's A/B switch now changes only the
drone; the claims about the drone are the ones that held up on 44 Sundays;
the men's verse no longer puts the basses above the tune; the costs are
given as ranges.*

## How to listen

The local server is running. Open a seed and press PLAY:

> http://127.0.0.1:8141/art/kolob/index.php?seed=17

- Change the number after `seed=` for another Sunday.
- The times below (mm:ss) are the page's own clock, shown beside each line of
  the clerk's minutes. They count from when you press PLAY. They were read
  from the test harness, and the browser keeps them: seed 17 in the browser
  turns the drone at 1:16 and begins the invocation at 1:19, as the harness
  does.
- **The programme card names the Sunday** (in Deseret, where it used to say
  ORDINARY, FAST DAY, CONFERENCE or JUBILEE). `&latin=1` shows it in
  English. The minutes' meeting row names it too: ☀ MEETING 1 · (the Sunday).
- **Every seed plays a different meeting than it did before this round.** The
  calendar changed the dice once, on purpose. From now on a seed plays the
  same meeting every time.

## What round 3b changed, by size

Honestly, from biggest to smallest.

1. **The ward sings the hymns. (The biggest change.)** Every hymn is now
   sung by thirty-two singers, each with a voice of their own, not by four
   synth voices. People come forward one or two at a time, and the minutes
   name them (✦): a child on the tune, a visitor who joins late, the old
   bass, the harmony alto, the enthusiast, the soloist's descant. The
   chorister gives the note before an unaccompanied hymn. Before a Sacred
   Harp tune, the sections "pitch" it: a chord of thirty-two voices builds
   up. The Old Way is lined out by the precentor, a man of the ward.
2. **An organist plays the hymns. (Big.)** Each Sunday seats one of three
   organists: a plain one, a Victorian or an improviser. The organist plays
   the pipe organ: gives out the tune, plays under every verse on the
   chorister's tempo, plays interludes and the amen, walks into a new key,
   and now and then plays a fill between two lines. On about one Sunday in
   five, the morning is the organist's prelude on the day's first hymn.
3. **A Sunday of the colony year. (Medium. You will notice it across visits
   more than within one.)** Each visit draws one of nine Sundays:
   - an ordinary Sunday (45 %);
   - a fast Sunday (15 %): testimony-heavy and sparse, the Sacred Harp and
     the Old Way, the plain organist, a grey dawn;
   - General Conference (12 %): three hymns, the Tabernacle, the Victorian
     organist;
   - Pioneer Day (8 %): the gospel ring and the brass bands;
   - Christmas (6 %): the handbells and the steeples, shape-note and Shaker
     songs, the Primary, a grey winter dawn;
   - Easter (6 %): the brightest light, the Tabernacle in full, the
     trombones;
   - a wedding (4 %): gentle, the soloist, the organ voluntary, the
     handbells; a late, already-awake morning;
   - a funeral (3 %): slow and dark at dawn, then rising into a Tabernacle
     doxology; no raspberry, no round, no refrain;
   - a dedication (1 %): the Tabernacle all day, the full organ.

   The Sunday decides the house style, how many hymns there are, which
   guests are likely, how the morning wakes and how bright the dawn is,
   which organist plays, and how many of the ward you come to know.
   Measured honestly: across the first three minutes of 30 seeds, the
   meetings are a little more different from each other than before round
   3b (see the handoff), but two ordinary Tabernacle Sundays can still sound
   alike at the start. The Sunday shows most in the hymns and guests later
   on.
4. **The rites between the hymns are seated. (Medium.)** The invocation, the
   testimony, the sacrament, an interlude and the postlude each draw how the
   house sits for them:
   - lined out only: the deacon's clarinet gives lines and the ward answers;
   - the brush arbor: no organ, the strings on bare fifths (its amen bowed
     too);
   - an organ voluntary: the organ leads;
   - the choir alone: the ward hums a few chords;
   - or the house as it always was.

   Two plain rites never come in a row. Because the sacrament keeps its
   stillness, the testimony before it is seated on most Sundays (nine in
   ten), and now spread over all four seatings (the choir alone about three
   testimonies in ten). The minutes name each one with a ⌖ row (for
   example ⌖ INVOCATION · LINED OUT ONLY). Whatever a voice that sits the
   rite out had begun in the joint before it now fades as the rite begins.
5. **Every style, and new forms. (Medium, occasional.)** All six styles are
   sung in the meeting: the Tabernacle, the Sacred Harp, New England
   psalmody (the fuging tunes), gospel, the Shaker and Primary songs, and the
   Old Way. On top of those come:
   - a round (about one meeting in five);
   - the wandering refrain: a short tune the enthusiast starts after the
     first hymn, which comes back later and in the doxology (about one
     meeting in four);
   - the partner hymn: the closing hymn written on the first hymn's chords,
     with the first hymn played against it (heard in about one meeting in
     eight);
   - gospel's standing quartet;
   - the Primary's children.
6. **Two new guests. (Occasional.)** The ward's handbell choir (about one
   meeting in eight) and the singing school: you arrive while the choir is
   still practising (about one Sunday in ten; experimental,
   `&exp=-singingSchool` turns it off).
7. **The arc of light. (Small, and gradual.)** A meeting now rises from dawn
   to full daylight:
   - The prelude and the invocation are plainer. The house organ is on its
     flutes, and the early hymns lean toward the Sacred Harp, the Old Way and
     the Shakers.
   - The sacrament is the stillest point.
   - The doxology is full light. It leans toward the Tabernacle and the
     gospel ring, and a Tabernacle doxology is written by one of the
     colony's hymnists who likes sevenths and borrowed chords.
   - The postlude is evening.

   How big, measured: the styles move clearly (at dawn the gospel ring is 8
   hymns in a hundred, in the morning 19, at full light 24; the Old Way 15,
   7, 3). The harmony moves only a little: the doxology has somewhat more
   seventh chords than the first two hymns (about 11 chords in a hundred
   against 7 or 8; a third hymn, on the Sundays that have one, has as many)
   and half as many bare fifths (7 against 15). The house organ's
   own chords reach the full organ in the doxology. The organist draws the
   full organ in about four doxologies in ten, not much more often than in
   the other hymns (three and a half in ten). Do not expect a sunrise; it is
   a slow brightening.

   The old jump in loudness and busyness at every joint is gone: each rite
   now fades in from where the last one ended. (Measured: the biggest
   one-second step fell from 0.35 to 0.12 on the engine's 0–1 scale.)
8. **The Kolob reckoning. (Small and subtle. It is for the attentive
   listener.)**
   - On about two Sundays in three, the drone no longer sits on one note all
     meeting. At each joint, usually while the organ plays the joint's amen,
     it glides over four to six seconds to a new note: one note per rite.
   - Those notes are the opening of the tune the doxology will sing. In the
     doxology the drone is home again, and the ward sings that tune at
     normal speed.
   - On about half of those Sundays the drone spells the tune's own first
     notes. On the other half it spells the tune's stressed notes (its
     skeleton: the notes on the strong syllables), which you will hear as
     the tune's outline rather than its opening.
   - The cantus is as long as the meeting has rites before the doxology:
     four to seven notes, not the seven to nine the plan hoped for.
   - Every drone note is the keynote, third or fifth of the key its rite is
     sung in, and the drone always moves (at least three different notes,
     counting the keynote it starts on).
   - On the other Sundays (about one in three) no written doxology fits the
     day's keys. The drone then stays on the keynote all meeting, as before.
   - The minutes print one row: ∿ THE DRONE'S TUNE, as the doxology begins
     (not when the switch below holds the drone at home).
   - **Honestly, by measurement:** a moving drone is a note of the chord
     sounding above it a little less often than the keynote drone (65 %
     against 69 % of the moments with a harmony, over 44 Sundays), mostly
     when it sits on the third. It clashes (a second against it) about as
     often (38 % either way). The weakest moment is the landing: as the
     glide ends, the amen's last chord, or a chord the strings are still
     holding from the rite before, is a second against the new note in about
     half the turns (53 %, against 39 % for the keynote drone at the same
     moments), and in three turns of four when the next hymn is sung in
     another key.
   - `&exp=-reckoning` turns it off for an A/B: **the same Sunday, the same
     hymns and the same doxology**, with the drone on the keynote all
     meeting. That is the one thing that changes.
9. **Small things.**
   - A verse given to the men or to the women, now and then (the chorister
     says so in the minutes). In the men's verse the tenors sing the tune an
     octave down and the basses stay under them: their own part where it
     lies below the tune, else an octave lower, else the tune with the
     tenors. In the Tabernacle's men's verses the basses mostly join the
     tune, so those verses are often in unison.
   - A refrain that comes back is sung out a little more each time.
   - The Hosanna has a hook for Easter and a dedication. It is not built
     yet and makes no sound.

**Not there yet:** words (the ward sings on vowels and on "fa sol la mi");
the testimony-bearers are seated but do not speak; the Hosanna, the Social
Hall, the far ward and the other guests in the plan's section 8 are not built.

What to read from it:

- **Over a whole meeting, round 3b costs about 1.1 to 1.4 times the audio
  work of the build before it** (1.4 to 1.5 times in the busy seconds).
  The one trace of the build before it is a single sample too.
- **The ward is the weight.** Thirty-two voices with a throat each take
  about twice the audio work of the four old voices in a hymn (step 1's
  measurement). The hymns hold 1,900–2,900 audio nodes (once 4,330), against
  230–1,100 in the rites around them. The organist, the guests, the
  calendar and the reckoning are small beside it.
- **The node counts are an upper bound.** Chrome's inspector counts a node
  until the browser clears it away, so the peak depends on when that
  happens. That is why the critic's run of the same Sunday reached 4,330
  where ours reached 2,100–2,600.
- **The worst moments are in the hymns, where the whole ward sings.**
  Chrome's render capacity reached 61–67 % there for a second, and one audio
  callback took 4.3–5.1 ms of its 5.3 ms. On this Mac, busy with other work,
  that is close to an audible glitch at the peaks. The build before round 3b
  had more headroom (its worst callback took 3.7 ms).
- **This step (the Sunday, the light, the seatings, the reckoning) adds
  almost nothing on the audio thread.** The drone's glide moves the
  frequencies of sines that were already sounding, and a hummed rite is a
  few seconds of the ward.
- **The reckoning's real cost is off the audio path.** The composer writes
  the doxology up to 24 ways, in its own thread, until one fits the day's
  keys. In the browser that took 156 ms (seed 17), about 400 ms (seed 9) and
  436 ms (seed 14, which tried all 24 and fell back), within seconds of
  pressing PLAY. The drone does not need it until the prelude has ended.
- **If you scale back, the obvious levers are:**
  - the ward's size, or a lighter throat for the inner parts;
  - the number of candidate doxologies, `RECKON_CANDIDATES` in
    `kolob-calendar.js`. It is 24 now (two Sundays in three reckon). At 12,
    about three in five would; at 1, only the Sundays where the composer's
    first doxology fits, one in eight or so;
  - the reckoning itself, with `&exp=-reckoning` (the switch's default
    lives in `kolob-experimental.js`).

## Known rough edges

- **The drone's landing.** See item 8: as a glide ends, the amen's last
  chord or a held string chord from the rite before often rubs against the
  new note for a few seconds, most of all before a hymn in another key. If
  it sounds wrong to you, the fix we would try first is to land the glide
  as the next rite begins, on its first chord. `&exp=-reckoning` is the
  fallback.
- **The Tabernacle's men's verses are mostly in unison**, because the
  Tabernacle's bass part often lies above the tune once the tenors take it
  down an octave.
- **The testimony is seated on most Sundays** (the rule, and the
  sacrament's stillness).
- **The arc of light is gentle**, as said above: styles and stops move
  clearly, the harmony only a little.
- **Guests keep their own time.** The trombone choir's known compass issue
  (seeds 17 and 37) is unchanged from before this round.
