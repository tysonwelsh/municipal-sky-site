## 1. Proposals

**1. The invocation prays: a chant on the drone.**
- *Idea.* The invocation stops waiting for a guest and gets seatings of its own, drawn per Sunday on `invocation:<n>`:
  - **The chant (about half of Sundays).** The drone's note becomes a reciting tone.
    - The choir recites on it in speech rhythm, on vowels until Deseret words exist.
    - Each half-verse closes on a harmonised formula: a mediation, then a termination.
    - After four to six verses, the whole ward says "amen", 32 voices not quite together.
  - **The Housatonic (about a quarter).** The strings' slow chorale forms a mist, and the clarinet sings the first hymn's head slowly through it.
    - The chorale is already built for the Question (`kolob-question.js`, `chorale()`) and has sat unused since the Question was shelved.
  - **Otherwise, the organ's quiet voluntary**, as now, but engraved.
- *Roots.*
  - Ives's *Psalm 90* (1924): the organ holds one low C under the whole piece, the choir recites over it, and bells ring at the close.
  - Chanting to a harmonised formula (Anglican chant), which Lowell Mason's tunebooks printed beside the hymn tunes.
  - The LDS custom that the whole congregation says "amen" aloud after every prayer.
  - Ives's *The Housatonic at Stockbridge*, where MISSIONARY CHANT rises through a mist of strings.
- *Heard.*
  - Every visit gets one of these at about 1:20–3:00. That is exactly where all three panel critics found the meeting empty.
  - The chant brings cadences the meeting lacks: half closes and V–I, not IV–I.
- *Crew and round.* Round 3.
  - FORM: the seat.
  - HYMN: the chant formulas, from Harmony.
  - CAST, later: the amen's 32 voices.
  - ENGRAVE: chant notation, with a breve for the reciting note. The plate already draws breves.
- *Cost.* Small to medium. The Housatonic only needs wiring up.
- *Risk.* It could sound like a cathedral rather than a frontier meetinghouse, or read as more drone. Keep it plain: no organ, bare fifths on brush-arbor Sundays, and a cadence at least every 15 s.

**2. The organist's prelude on the day's first hymn** (the Variety critic's proposal, passed on to this brief).
- *Idea.* On about 40 % of Sundays with no trombones and no withheld tune, the prelude is a chorale prelude on the first hymn, in the organist's style:
  - the plain organist plays it through on soft flutes;
  - the Victorian decorates the tune with suspensions;
  - the improviser puts the tune in the pedals under running figures, and rarely in a second key.
- *Roots.*
  - LDS prelude practice: organists play hymn arrangements before the meeting.
  - The Lutheran chorale prelude, which existed to introduce the congregation's chorale (Bach's *Orgelbüchlein*).
  - Ives, a church organist until 1902, and his *Adeste Fideles in an Organ Prelude*.
- *Heard.* A 30–50 s prelude between about 0:05 and 1:00. About two minutes later the choir sings the same tune, and the listener recognises it (design law 2).
- *Crew and round.* After HYMN's first milestone; until then it would only play the poured theme.
  - CAST (the organist's styles), with FORM, reusing `dawnChorale`.
  - ENGRAVE prints it.
- *Cost.* Small for the plain version; medium for the pedal tune and the running figures.
- *Risk.* It could become the new fixed opening. Keep it a draw, and check it with `opentwin.js` against the Variety critic's test: no entry time shared by more than 25 % of seeds.

**3. The singing school: the choir is still practising.**
- *Idea.* On some Sundays you arrive during choir practice.
  1. The chorister strikes the tuning fork, and the choir sings the first line of the day's first hymn.
  2. One part goes plainly wrong: the tenors climb where the tune turns down, or the altos come in a beat late.
  3. The chorister cuts them off, and that part sings the passage alone, slowly, "on the notes".
  4. Everyone sings it again from the pickup, through to the cadence.

  At the first hymn they get it right.
- *Roots.*
  - The American singing school, where the tunebooks came from. Billings's *New-England Psalm-Singer* (1770) opens with the rudiments for singing classes, and Sacred Harp singing schools still run today.
  - The singing classes of pioneer Utah.
  - The ward choir's practice just before sacrament meeting.
- *Heard.* In the prelude, about one Sunday in ten, for 30–50 s. It pays off at about 2:50, when the first hymn comes in correctly.
- *Crew and round.* GUEST, one agent, round 3, using today's choir voice. It needs a cut-off mid-line and a way to sing one part alone. CAST later names the chorister and the tenor.
- *Cost.* Small to medium.
- *Risk.* It could sound like a glitch or a stuck loop. Allow only one correction, make the wrong note a real clash rather than a comma off, and cut off on a breath. Never at a funeral.

**4. The wandering refrain.**
- *Idea.* A two-line refrain in the camp-meeting lilt belongs to the meeting rather than to any one hymn.
  - After the first hymn's last verse, the enthusiast starts it alone, and the ward has it by the second time through.
  - It comes back after a later hymn, in that hymn's key and dialect.
  - In the doxology the ward sings it without anyone starting it.
- *Roots.*
  - Camp meetings, from Cane Ridge (1801) on, fastened wandering refrains onto older hymns. That is how PROMISED LAND, already in Kolob's tune book, got "I am bound for the promised land".
  - Ives's Violin Sonata No. 4, *Children's Day at the Camp Meeting*, built on "Shall We Gather at the River", and his Third Symphony, *The Camp Meeting*.
  - The Saints' own shouting chorus in "The Spirit of God".
- *Heard.* In about one meeting in four: at about 5:30, again after the last hymn, and in the doxology, brighter each time. It is the meeting's own tune, sung by people in the room.
- *Crew and round.* Round 4.
  - HYMN writes a refrain whose cadence fits every key of the day.
  - CAST: the enthusiast starts it.
  - FORM places it at the joints.
- *Cost.* Medium.
- *Risk.* It could turn the meeting into a revival. Never on fast Sundays or at funerals, three statements at most, and it counts as the doxology's one payoff (see §2).

**5. The ward's handbell choir.**
- *Idea.* The first guest who stands in the room.
  - Eight to twelve ringers, each with two or three bells, stand in a line from low to high, as real handbell choirs do. So the tune hops across the stereo field as it rises and falls.
  - They ring the day's hymn, or one of the approved rounds.
  - They use real techniques: bells left to ring or damped, martellato, a shake on long notes, and a cascade down the line to finish.
- *Roots.*
  - Handbells were made so tower ringers could practise their changes indoors.
  - Margaret Shurcliff's Beacon Hill ringers (Boston, 1923) started the American handbell choir.
  - Many LDS wards keep a handbell choir, and so does the Tabernacle Choir (Bells at Temple Square).
  - A handbell's tuned twelfth gives every bell a pure fifth in Kolob's tuning.
- *Heard.* Close, bright and dry: full daylight.
  - In about one meeting in eight: in the invocation, before the sacrament, or in the postlude.
  - It leans toward Christmas, Easter and weddings, and never shares a meeting with the steeples.
- *Crew and round.* GUEST, one agent, round 3.
  - The voice already exists (`kolob-voices-folk.js`, `handbells`).
  - It engraves as ringed heads, like the bells.
- *Cost.* Small.
- *Risk.* Too many bells in one meeting: the bells layer, the steeples and the Primary. Allow one bell guest per meeting, and for phones cap how many bells ring on, at about 12 nodes a strike.

## 2. What this round revealed

  - The page is blank 83–100 % of the time.

  The fix is proposal 1. Until then, let guests seat in the invocation, and have ENGRAVE print the organ when it plays alone (ENGRAVING §4.6).

- **Every delight so far comes from far away:** the old tune, the far choir, the steeples, the bands, and next the far ward and the handcart. The best moments the panel measured are near and bright: the house's return on seed 1847 at 0:40 (+9 dB in the 300 Hz–4 kHz band), and the bands at 13:36 on seed 27.
  - Weight the next guest round toward the room: the Primary, the Social Hall, proposals 3–5.

  - The old tune is as loud as the hymn, so lower it 6–8 dB. Give it a 50 ms dip at each onset, so repeated notes are re-struck (76 of 367 steps are repeated notes).
- **Guests are only as good as their material.** The trombones' top line uses a median of 5 pitches, and 75 of 117 phrases share their first five notes. Build the composer before any more guests that play "the day's hymn".

Make it a SCORE guest rule: every entrance releases the house's held notes over about 1.5 s.
- **Cadences.**
  - The plagal share is 80.5–82.3 %, against a target of 30–55 %. The dialects remain the main fix; proposal 1 helps.
  - Keep the trombones' final I, but put a V before it (11 of 24 reach it from vi, a suspension or I itself).
