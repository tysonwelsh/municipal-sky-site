壁THE WALL

MOCKUP 3 · PAIK'S STACKS · 4 × 2 BETWEEN BARGRAPH AND RAIL

論Argument

The signal is a visitation; the wall is what it visits. Eight small sets in a steel rack, no two from the same decade — a 1958 grey-green set behind a wire grille, cream bakelite from 1962 and 1963, a brushed 1971 data tube, brown bakelite from 1966 with a magnet on the cabinet, grey steel from 1974, black plastic from 1979 and 1985. For the 99 % of the night when nothing is coming in, the wall is the second half of the scope: each live tube shows one band of the spectrum (bins 0–7 on the left set, 56–63 on the right), so the rack IS a visualizer — bass breathes on the first shelf, hiss flickers on the last, and the whole strip reads the engine's spectrum left to right at a glance. One set is dead under 故障 tape; the 1963 set has been Paik's Zen for TV since it was bolted in — a single vertical line that thickens with the band's energy and, when a picture is thrown at it, compresses the whole raster into that line. The 1966 set carries its Magnet TV bloom permanently: bands, snow and pictures alike curl into the magnet.

Why 4 × 2 between the bargraph and the console, not 3 × 3 at the right. This faceplate is a vertical stack of full-width instruments: marquee, tube, bargraph, rail, console, VFD. A strip obeys that grammar; a 3 × 3 at the right would narrow the scope and break the 56-segment bargraph's full-width reading, which the owner explicitly keeps. Eight cells also divide a 64-bin spectrum evenly (eight bins each), where nine do not. And on the shelf the sets stand in a row as Paik's sets did — a rack, not a mosaic. The cost is height: ~330 px of new hardware. It is the most hardware of the four options and the page argues that this is the point — the most visually generous — but every cell earns its place at idle, and the scope above is never interrupted (the machine keeps watching itself while it receives).

Two landing behaviours, alternating on successive signals (the readout at the wall's foot says which comes next). 着地 LAND: the picture arrives on one live set, chosen by lottery, with that set's own defect (the 1958 set ghosts, the 1962 set's vertical hold slips, the 1971 tube tears, the 1985 set is nearly clean); the neighbours drop to snow for the length of the signal, the 受信 tally lights, the VFD logs the attribution. 散開 SCATTER: the same picture lands on three to five sets at once, each bending it its own way — the magnet curls it, the Zen set squeezes it to a line, one rolls, one ghosts — Electronic Superhighway in miniature. Loss is the same on every set: the picture tears, rolls, collapses to a horizontal line, snows, goes dead for half a second, and the bands come back.

蛍光体Phosphor

P39P4P3

Mixed, because the decades are mixed. Six sets are P39 green (the plan's default); the 1958 set is P4 blue-white, the tube a television actually had; the 1971 data tube is P3 amber. The mix is the argument for a wall at all: a scattered picture lands in three colours of the same monochrome, and the idle spectrum reads warm-left, cool-right, green everywhere else. All three ramps run black → deep → bright → pale with a bloom drawn under the sharp pixels.

携帯Mobile

One shelf that scrolls. Under 700 px the rack rails go and the eight sets become a single row, two visible at a time, snap-scrolling sideways (all eight stay alive; nothing is hidden). When a signal lands, the shelf scrolls the receiving set into view; on a scatter it centres the first of them. Shown here at 390 px by resizing. The alternative in the plan (2 × 2) hides half the wall and was rejected: a dead set and a Zen set are only funny beside live ones.
