MAP OPTION M3 · 夜図 THE NIGHT’S CHART the map remembers

The argument. A signal here is a visitation: five to fifteen seconds of somewhere else, and then it is gone. A map that lights a dot and then forgets it gives that visitation the same lifespan it already had — you would have to be looking at the strip at the right second for it to mean anything, and the strip would be dark for the other fifty-five. So this one remembers. Every reel received tonight leaves a mark, and the marks do not vanish; they cool down the phosphor ramp and stay. After twenty minutes the strip is the shape of the night — clustered on Japan, or scattered over four continents, or thin because it has been a quiet hour. That is the thing none of the other options can give: a reason to look at the strip when nothing is playing, which is most of the time. The live dot is still the brightest thing on it; everything behind it is the evening’s wake.

THE PROJECTION — AND WHY IT IS NOT SYMMETRIC

Equirectangular, full 360° of longitude, +70° to −45° of latitude — 115° of latitude, so the ribbon is 3.13 : 1, exactly as wide-and-short as the ±57.5° the brief asked for, and it loses nothing. The pool runs from Igloolik, Nunavut at 69.38° N (two Inuit Broadcasting reels) down to Auckland at 41.29° S. A symmetric ±58 crop would have the same aspect and would throw three real receptions off the top of the chart; it would also behead Scandinavia, Alaska and most of Canada, which makes the strip less recognisable as the world, not more. So the crop is set by the machine’s own reach rather than by symmetry, with a little margin at each end. Anything that ever falls outside it is clamped to the edge row and drawn as a horizontal dash lying on the rail rather than as a dot in the wrong place. Nothing in the present pool does.

The ribbon is a second tube, not a graphic: the same P39 long-persistence phosphor as the television, the same LUT, scanlines every other cell row, a bloom pass at the raster’s own size, a slow vertical jitter, and glass over the top. Land is a coarse grid of square cells — … — rasterised at runtime from hand-drawn coastline polygons, so the cell size stays about 2 px whatever width the strip is given. The sea carries a faint graticule (the equator, and meridians every 30°) at a third of the land’s value.

HOW MARKS AGE

Cooling, in five steps down the phosphor ramp. Not shrinking and not drifting — a mark holds its place and its size for the whole night and only loses heat, because the one thing the chart must never do is make an old reception ambiguous about where it was. The ramp is the tube’s own, so the whole strip reads as one piece of glass:

THE THING THAT ACTUALLY DECIDES WHETHER THIS WORKS: 密集 STACKING

The pool is not spread evenly. Thirteen reels are Pyongyang. Ten are Tokyo. Six are London, six are Lhasa. At 488 px those are the same pixel and at 390 px they are the same pixel twice over, so a chart that just lights a dot per reception turns into one hot blob over East Asia within the hour — a record of nothing. The answer here is that a site is not a dot, it is a rosette: each reception at a site takes its own cell on a fixed outward spiral, freshest at the heart, older ones pushed out ring by ring. Thirteen receptions at Pyongyang is a five-cell rosette with a bright core and a cold rim — you can count it, and you can see the order you heard them in. 密集 STACK TEST on the bench fires every Pyongyang and every Tokyo reel at once so the worst case is on screen in a second rather than after an hour of waiting.

THE TWO SPECIAL CASES

Caught at distance (15 reels, dx: true). A DX catch is a transmitter picked up far outside its service area. Fifteen reels carry the flag but only six name where the recordist sat, so only six can honestly be drawn as two points. The other nine get the dot at the transmitter plus a faint halo ring three cells out — the chart’s way of saying this one came from further away than it looks without inventing a receiver.

The six arcs. ph-tvdx-abscbn-1990 (Quezon City → Tokyo), pk-tvdx-ptv-1994, wwv-fort-collins-1991 (Fort Collins → Rockford), radio-moscow-parade-1969 (Red Square → Brisbane), sovetskie-kv-stantsii-1970 (Moscow → Ontario) and kol-israel-9009-1969 (Jerusalem → the eastern United States, vague on purpose, so it is drawn at country softness). The arc bows poleward, is dashed at cell resolution, wraps the ±180 seam when that is the short way round, and persists and cools with its two ends. While the reel is on the air a bright dash travels along it from transmitter to receiver. Six is the right number for a chart that accumulates: enough that one is an event, few enough that the strip never becomes a cat’s cradle.

Off Earth (7 reels). There is no honest dot for the Moon on a world map, so the chart does not pretend: these are drawn at their Earth anchor — the ground station, relay or launch site named in geo.json — with a dashed mast rising out of the top of the frame and a cap on the rail whose width says which realm it was: one cell 軌道 orbit (4), two cells 月 moon (2), three cells 深宇宙 deep space (1). The rule is the same one the arcs obey — the bright end is where it was heard — so the mast is just the extreme case of an arc whose far end is off the chart. It persists like everything else: an hour later the mast is a cold dotted line, which is the correct feeling. nasa-mercury-redstone-1-1960 has no realm and gets no mast: that flight never left the pad.

DOES IT SURVIVE A RELOAD?

Yes, for six hours. The chart is kept in localStorage as reel ids and absolute timestamps, so reloading restores it with the cooling correct rather than resetting everything to new. Entries older than six hours are dropped on load: a night is a night. Every access is wrapped in try/catch — a private window, blocked site data or a quota refusal simply means the strip starts empty, the chin says 記録不可, and nothing else about the page changes. 消去 in the chin clears it; it arms on the first press and clears on a second within two seconds, because wiping the night by mis-clicking would be the one unforgivable bug in this option. CAN YOU READ A MARK?

Hover or tap anywhere on the strip and the chin names the nearest reception within a few cells: the reel, the place, the confidence, and how long ago. With the strip focused, ← and → step through the night newest-first and Home jumps to the live one; the mark being read gets a locator ring. That is the whole interaction — no panel, no legend, no zoom. It is a chart, not a control surface.

IDLE

Idle is the point of this option, and the accumulated chart is the idle state — but the ribbon still moves on its own. A receiver sweep crosses it left to right about every fourteen seconds, lifting the cells it passes and briefly flaring the marks it crosses, so the night gets re-read in front of you. The terminator is real: the sub-solar longitude is computed from the clock, and the daylit third of the strip sits about 40 % down while the night side runs full — the ionosphere closes at dawn, which is also when the machine stops hearing anything. The land carries the tube’s vertical jitter and the odd thermal spark. Nothing here is decoration that could be switched off without the strip looking dead.
