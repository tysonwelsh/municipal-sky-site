THE SOURCE

The Ashcroft 6-inch boiler gauge is the brass eye on the backhead of every American locomotive and on the front of every stationary engine from the 1880s to the 1920s — Crosby and Dewrance built the same object in England. A cast brass case, a screwed-on bezel ring with a knurled edge and two fine-slotted screws at twelve and six, a thick bevelled glass, and behind it a porcelain-enamel dial with engraved Didone figures around a full 270 degrees, a hand-painted red line at the working pressure, and a slim blued-steel spade pointer on a brass boss. A quarter-inch pipe stub leaves the bottom of the case.

The tells kept here: the figures run from 0 at about seven o'clock to the top of the range at about five, each one rotated upright to its own radius; the red line is painted by hand, so it wobbles, is fatter at the inner end, and bleeds a little where the brush lifted; the maker's cartouche is an oval of fine engraved rules with a city line, deliberately generic; the pointer is a slim shaft that swells to a leaf four millimetres before the tip, with a short tail and a round brass counterweight; the bezel has a radially hatched knurl, a polished flat face and a warm reflection at its upper left; the enamel is not white but faintly warm toward the rim, crazed with hairlines, and chipped down to the iron in two or three places, one of them under the screw at six. The pointer has Bourdon mass: it overshoots by about five per cent, bounces once more, and then trembles a third of a degree at six hertz as the steam pulses.

Left out: the rear threads and the syphon pipe; the real maker's name and eagle (a generic cartouche stands in); the Didone is Playfair Display rather than a drawn Scotch Roman, with Libre Franklin carrying the small caps.
