THE SOURCE

The cast-brass bell push screwed to the jamb of every late-Victorian front door: a 55 mm escutcheon with a rope-twist rim, two slotted screws, and a small dome of mother-of-pearl in a brass collar for the thumb. Kenrick and Comyn Ching sold them by the gross; the American equivalent is the Bryant pattern. Some are engraved PRESS in Roman capitals above the dome; the oval tradesmen's push beside it is plain.

The tells kept here: the rope-twist rim is twenty-eight separate beads, each one lit on the side that faces the light above-left and dark on the other, so the ring turns from bright at the top-left to nearly black at the bottom-right — the thing a flat ring of notches never does; the grooves between them hold a green-brown tarnish; the polished field carries one soft, broad reflection of the room rather than a mirror; the pearl is soft — a wide low highlight with a blue and a pink flare underneath it, never a chrome hot-spot — and its top is glossier than its sides, a century of thumbs; the brass immediately around the collar is polished lighter for the same reason; the two slotted screws sit at odd angles because nobody ever lined them up. PRESS is engraved: dark fill with a lit lower edge. The dome sinks 2 px in 60 ms and takes 140 ms to come back, the way a sprung brass plunger does.

Left out: the lacquer's crazing, the bell wire and the wooden pattress behind it, and the hand-chased detail that varies push to push — this one is a catalogue casting, cleanly struck.
