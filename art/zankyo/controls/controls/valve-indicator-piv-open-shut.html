THE SOURCE

A post-indicator valve stands in the grass outside every sprinklered building in America: a red cast-iron post over the gate valve on the buried main, with a little glass window near the top. Behind the glass a target plate carries the words OPEN and SHUT, cast in raised letters and painted white on black, and it slides as the valve's stem turns. The fire marshal reads the window from the road; the hasp beside it takes a padlock so nobody shuts the main quietly.

The tells kept here: the post is a casting, not a tube — a flared cap, two raised ribs down the face, and a surface that has been repainted over rust more than once, with chips down to grey iron on the cap edge and rust bleeding from under the window bolts; the window's own cast frame with four square-headed bolts and a single hard reflection across the glass that is not the shape of anything behind it; the letters are raised, so each one is lit on top and casts a line of shadow underneath; a brass padlock, dulled, on the hasp. The travel is slow and linear — about a second and a half from word to word, because a gate valve is many turns of a wrench — and it settles with the smallest bounce at the end.

Left out: the wrench stored on the post, the base flange and its bolts, and the maker's name cast in the cap.
