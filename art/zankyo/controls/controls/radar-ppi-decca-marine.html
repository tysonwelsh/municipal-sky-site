THE SOURCE

The Decca RM 916 and its ancestor the Decca 101 are the radar sets on every British-built ship's bridge from the late fifties on: a big round long-persistence tube sunk into a grey console under a black rubber viewing hood, with the bearing scale engraved 0–360 around the glass, four range rings, a heading marker straight up, and a bearing cursor (the EBL) and a variable range marker (the VRM) you crank onto a target to read its bearing and distance off the plates below.

The tells kept here: the two-colour persistence — the sweep paints each return as a cold blue-white flash that turns yellow-green within a breath and then fades over one whole revolution, so every echo is a blue tip on a yellow tail and a wedge of brightness follows the sweep round; the sweep line itself as a thin bright radius with a soft bloom; a ragged coastline down one side with the radar shadow behind the headlands; sea clutter as a speckled disc round the centre that is re-randomised on every pass; ships that creep a few pixels a revolution; the dashed EBL and the dotted VRM ring; the rubber hood as a dark soft-edged tunnel; and the engraved white bearing ring with 000 at the top. The phosphor lives on a canvas with a per-frame decay, so setting value sixty times a second costs three arc strokes.

Left out: the mechanical bearing cursor and its perspex arm, the range/brilliance/gain knobs down the side of the console, the interscan and the true-motion gearbox, and the ship's name on the maker's plate.
