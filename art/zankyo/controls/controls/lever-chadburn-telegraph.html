THE SOURCE

The Chadburn's engine-order telegraph stood on the bridge of every British steamship from the 1870s: a lacquered brass pedestal with a domed glass over a white enamelled dial, the orders painted in wedge-shaped sectors — AHEAD in black down the right, ASTERN in red down the left, STOP at the top — and a brass handle with a turned black grip that is the pointer. The officer swings the handle into a sector, a bell rings in the engine room, and the engineer swings his own handle to match; his answer comes back as the thin black reply pointer under the bridge handle, and the bell stops when the two agree.

The tells kept here: the heavy brass bezel with six slotted screws and a half-width detent notch at every position; the mirrored face with black radials between the sectors and the words set along the radials; the grip as a turned barrel, fatter in the middle, with a ferrule at each end and a polish where the hand goes; the thin reply pointer that lags the order by 400 ms and eases in; a small bell dome at the base of the pedestal that shivers while the order and the reply disagree; tarnish in the bezel's recess and one wide reflection across the upper-left of the glass. The handle follows the pointer's angle and snaps into the nearest sector with a small overshoot; the wheel steps a sector; arrows step, Shift-arrows run to the end. extended adds STAND BY and FINISHED WITH ENGINES at the bottom corners.

Left out: the second (engine-room) dial on the back of the head, the maker's name (a plain "SHIP TELEGRAPH" instead), the pedestal column below the neck.
