THE SOURCE

The Federal Sign & Signal Beacon Ray, Model 17 and its 173 cousin, is the chrome-and-red "gumball" that sat on every American patrol car, ambulance and fire chief's sedan from the late 1940s on: a cast chrome base with a flared skirt and a knurled ring, a 7-inch red glass dome moulded with vertical beehive flutes, and inside it a carrier turning at about 90 rpm carrying two sealed-beam lamps in parabolic reflectors, back to back — so the beam passes you twice a turn.

The tells kept here: the beam is not a blink, it is a lobe that crosses the dome, brightest when a reflector squares up to you and dying away as it turns, its brightness following cos² of the angle; the flutes chop that lobe into bright bars and crowd toward the dome's edges by true cylinder projection (x = R·sin θ, width ∝ cos θ); the dome is translucent, so unlit you can still see the reflectors, the lamp bases and the carrier shaft as dark shapes inside it; the chrome base carries a hard horizon line with sky above and ground below; the top cap is a small flat disc with a screw; and the flash spills out — a warm cast on the chrome, a pool on the panel, and a halo in the air that moves with the beam.

Left out: the gear noise, the sealed-beam's filament detail, the rubber gasket at the dome foot and the maker's full badge (a plain etched legend stands in for it). The phase attribute is a demo convenience — it only sets where the carrier starts, so a still screenshot can be taken with a beam facing the camera.
