THE SOURCE

The Grundig dial glass of the mid-fifties is the long warm window across the front of a walnut table radio: cream-gold glass lit by a row of pilot bulbs behind it, four bands stacked as horizontal gold lines (UKW, KW, MW, LW), frequency figures under each line and the names of Europe's transmitters above them, screen-printed in dark green and dark red condensed capitals. A single red wire crosses the whole height on a string drive and moves with a little slack; tiny lamps below show which band is switched in.

The tells kept here: the four band lines with names above and figures below, in two colours and two rows where the short-wave stations crowd; the pool of light that is brighter at the lower corners where the bulbs sit and dimmer in the middle; the glass's one wide reflection shaped by the room and the slightly washed look of the print under it; the band names at the left edge with the active one lit; the row of four lamps under the scale, one lit; the pointer that eases to its place over 200 ms and hesitates for 1 % of the scale when it changes direction. Drag the pointer or the glass, use the wheel or arrows, or the small flywheel knob at the right (the knob attribute), which coasts.

Left out: the metre-band markings on the KW line, the piano-key band switch, the magic-eye tuning indicator that sat beside the glass.
