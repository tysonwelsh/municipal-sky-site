THE SOURCE

The Edwards 340 — and its Faraday and Simplex cousins — is the gong screwed to the corridor wall of every American school, mill and hospital built between the wars and the seventies: a spun-steel gong of six or ten inches painted signal red, a red cast back box smaller than the gong, a solenoid or motor-driven hammer hidden behind it whose head just shows past the rim at four o'clock, and a run of half-inch conduit dropping into the top of the box.

The tells kept here: the gong is domed — a broad highlight up at the top-left, a dark crescent at the bottom-right, a band of reflected light on the underside of the rolled rim; the central boss with its hex bolt; FIRE cast into the face and painted over, so it is an emboss with no ink of its own; a chip on the rim down to bare steel where a mop handle found it, and a lighter scuff above it; the conduit stub and its cast hub; the back box peeking below the gong. Ringing, the gong shakes about two pixels at twenty hertz and the hammer alternates so fast it doubles; a single strike is a sixty-millisecond swing and a four-hundred millisecond ring-down.

Left out: the rear solenoid and its terminals, the mounting screws hidden under the gong, and the maker's name — the cast word is the generic FIRE, not a trademark.
