THE SOURCE

Every American studio of the 1930s had one of these over the door: a shop-made steel box about twelve inches by five, a stepped Deco frame of three concentric ledges, and a face of red glass with the letters left clear — the glass is painted red on the back and the letters are scraped out of the paint, so they are holes in the red, not a legend printed on a tile. Two lamps inside, wired to the transmitter's relay. It hangs off the wall on a bracket and throws a shadow down the plaster.

The tells kept here: the three ledges, each with its riser in shadow at the top-left and lit at the bottom-right the way a recess is; the two lamps as two hot spots with a cooler band between them and dark corners; the letters as clear glass — dark maroon ghosts when the box is out, white with a warm bloom when it is lit; one broad diagonal reflection across the glass that has nothing to do with the lettering under it; the red light spilling onto the frame's inner ledges and onto the wall around the box. The lamps are incandescent: they take about 200 ms to come up, the second one 40 ms behind the first, and about 400 ms to die. The lettering is hand-cut, so the stroke widths are not quite equal from letter to letter.

Left out: the conduit and the flexible cord, the ventilation louvres in the top of the box, the glass's ripple. A long label (including Cyrillic, with lang="ru") is squeezed to fit rather than run onto a second line — the real boxes were made to the word.
