THE SOURCE

The EMS VCS3 of 1969 — and the Synthi A that folded the same synthesiser into a briefcase — replaced the patch cord with a pin matrix: a small removable board drilled with 16 × 16 = 256 sockets, let into a black anodised plate, with the sources printed down the left edge and the destinations printed across the top. A connection is a pin pushed into the crossing of the two. The pins were sold in colours that were really resistances: white 2k7 (the standard patch), red 1 % (accurate control voltages) and green 68 k (attenuating). The same idea went up to 60 × 60 on the Synthi 100.

The tells kept here: the 256 holes on an exact 6.35 mm pitch, each a black dot with a chamfered mouth whose lit crescent sits at the lower right, so an empty board reads as a field of tiny highlights; the pin as a short cylinder standing proud — a domed top with one specular, a dark side, a moulding flash line, and a hard cast shadow down-right; pins wider than their holes; white pins common, red and green loud and rare; the legends silk-screened on the edges in condensed capitals, the columns rotated; fine rules grouping every fourth row and column; a faintly brushed anodised plate with the matrix board let into it; and a few sockets worn bright where pins have been pulled thousands of times. A hover shows a 40 % ghost pin so the target is legible; hovering a seated pin lifts it a pixel.

Left out: the board's physical removability (the real one lifts out on two thumb catches), the resistance values printed on the pin tray, and the sound of the pin seating.
