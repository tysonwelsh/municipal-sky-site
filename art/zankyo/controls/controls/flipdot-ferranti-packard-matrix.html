THE SOURCE

Kenyon Taylor's flip-disc, built by Ferranti-Packard in Toronto from 1961, is the sign that made a noise. Each dot is a disc of aluminium painted fluorescent yellow on one face and black on the other, pivoted on a wire through its middle with a tiny permanent magnet inside; a pulse through a coil behind the board flips it and it stays there with no power at all. The Montreal Stock Exchange board was the first; then airport gate signs, highway matrix signs, and every transit destination board that clatters when the bus changes route.

The tells kept here: the discs are a little smaller than their holes, so a dark ring surrounds each one, and the pivot pin shows as a bright speck at three o'clock on every disc; the yellow is matte fluorescent paint with no specular, shaded only top to bottom; the black reverse keeps a faint rim light. A change does not happen all at once — it sweeps across the board column by column, each disc turning about its vertical pivot in about forty milliseconds, so a new word arrives as a ripple of thin edge-on discs. Two discs per module are seeded sticky and arrive eighty milliseconds late, which is what a real board always does. The board is dead matte black with a fine grain, the frame is proud with four slotted screws, and every twenty-eight columns there is the dark gap between two modules.

Left out: the sound, which is the best part; the coils and the diode matrix behind the board; and the slight variation in paint age from disc to disc.
