THE SOURCE

The HP-35 of 1972 put a scientific calculator in a shirt pocket, and the way it did the display is the reason it is remembered: a strip of HP 5082-7400-series red LED clusters, each digit a die only a millimetre or so across, with a row of moulded bubble magnifiers over them and a dark red window in front. The whole window is 68 mm long; this build is ×4, which is the only way the hairline strokes are visible at all.

The tells kept here: fifteen bubbles on an 18 px pitch, each with a dark rim and one crescent glint in the upper left; the digits are drawn through the lens — every segment vertex is run through a barrel warp about the dome centre and magnified about 1.26×, so the middle of the digit swells and the corners lag, exactly as a plano-convex bubble does; the strokes are hairlines with 45° mitred ends, a 5° lean, and wide corner gaps, with a hotter core line down the middle of each; the decimal point lives inside the digit's own bubble at lower right, which is the HP quirk everybody recognises; the layout is a sign position, ten mantissa digits, a blank, then the exponent sign and two exponent digits right-justified; the red window sinks the unlit segments almost to nothing while the domes still glint; and the switching is instant — LEDs do not fade, so there is no transition anywhere in the element.

Left out: the multiplex flicker and the individual dice's slightly different reds, the cluster's gold bond wires seen under the dome at a low angle, and the HP wordmark (a plain silk-screened part number stands in for it).
