THE SOURCE

William Marvy of St Paul built more barber poles than anyone — some fifty thousand of them — and the Model 55 is the one everybody pictures: a clear glass cylinder with a printed red-white-blue helix turning inside it on a slow motor, chrome-plated castings top and bottom, and a frosted glass globe over the lamp on top. The helix only turns; the stripes appear to climb for ever, which is the whole trick.

The tells kept here: the stripes are a real cylindrical projection, not a skewed pattern — each boundary is the curve x = R·sin θ, so the stripes crowd toward the edges and run almost vertical there while they lie diagonal across the middle; a fixed shading over them, dark at both edges with a highlight band just left of centre, and two fixed glass streaks the stripes travel under; one full red-white-blue cycle every ninety pixels of height; the chrome castings with flutes that crowd at their edges too, and a hard horizon across them; the globe warm and haloed when the lamp is on, dead grey-white when it is off; the wall bracket behind with its two bolts. Switching on, the motor takes about a second and a half to come up to speed and coasts for two when it goes off — the light is instant by comparison.

Left out: the Marvy name on the casting (a generic model plate instead), the mounting arm's full length, and the cylinder's lower drain slot.
