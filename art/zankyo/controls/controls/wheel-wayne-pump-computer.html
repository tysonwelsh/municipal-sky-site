THE SOURCE

The computing pump arrived in the late 1930s and by the 1950s every Wayne 60/70 and Tokheim 39 on a roadside had this face: a cream porcelain-enamelled casting, a chromed bezel over each window, and behind the glass two rows of white numeral drums — THIS SALE $ above, GALLONS below — geared to the meter, with the price per gallon set on a small pair of wheels at the bottom. Lift the handle and the gallons wheels crawl while the money wheels run away from them.

The tells kept: the drums are projected cylinders, not a sliding strip — each numeral is placed by angle, y = R·sin θ, squashed by cos θ, so figures foreshorten and crowd at the top and bottom of the window while the next one rolls in from below; true odometer carry, where a wheel only moves in the last tenth of the wheel to its right, so carries snap; the cents wheel doubles into a 40 % ghost when it spins fast; the deep window with its cylinder shading and the fine dark seam between wheels; the red decimal panel in each row; cream enamel yellowed toward the edges with a chip or two of exposed steel; a chromed bezel and bowed glass with one long highlight. reset() spins everything back to zero in half a second, the way lifting the handle does.

Left out: the pump body, the globe and the hose; the maker's name (a generic face, to avoid a trademark); the mechanical clatter, and the tiny "9/10" that really followed the price.
