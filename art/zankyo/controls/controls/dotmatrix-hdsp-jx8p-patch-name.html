THE SOURCE

The HP HDSP-2000 family are "smart" alphanumeric LED displays: four characters of 5×7 red dots in one plastic DIP, with the shift register and the character ROM on the die under the window. String four packages end to end and you have the sixteen characters that told a Roland JX-8P owner in 1985 which patch they were on; stack two rows of them and you have an Oberheim Matrix-12. HP put the same part in frequency counters and logic analysers.

The tells kept here: the dots are tiny, round, individually distinct and crisp — the opposite of a smeared Panaplex — each with a hot pale core and a small bloom; the unlit dots are still faintly there as dark red ghosts, because the filter never quite hides them. Every four characters the black behind the filter changes and the gap widens by one dot column: that is the edge of the next DIP. The glyph table is the HDSP ROM's compact face — a plain (unslashed) zero, an M whose centre stroke only dips to row 3, and a real lowercase with descenders that fit inside the seven rows. The window is a dark red filter with one broad diagonal reflection, and the panel carries a crisp white silk-screened legend and hairline box.

Left out: the DIP's pins and the PCB behind them, the HDSP's own character-ROM quirks beyond the three above, and the blanking that a real display shows while the shift register clocks.
