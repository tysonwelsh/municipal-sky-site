THE SOURCE

The General Electric Type I-50 is the meter on the side of an American house from about 1950 to the end of the century: an induction motor whose rotor is a horizontal aluminium disc, dragged round by the current in the house and braked by two permanent magnets, so that the disc's speed is exactly proportional to the power being drawn. Everything sits under a clear glass dome clamped to a black base and closed with a lead seal on a wire. The constant printed on the nameplate — Kh 7.2 — is the watt-hours per revolution: count the turns for a minute, multiply, and you know what the house is using without touching anything.

The tells kept here: five small dials in a row reading 10000 · 1000 · 100 · 10 · 1, each with a tiny black arrow, and alternating in direction — the units dial clockwise, the tens anticlockwise, and so on, because the gear train reverses at every stage; the reading taken from each pointer's last-passed figure. Below them the disc, seen as a thin bright ellipse slightly from above, turned with a spun sheen, with the black mark on its rim coming round once per revolution and foreshortening away at the sides — the one motion cue on the whole instrument. The dome carries one vertical highlight band, a second curved one at the lower right, a green tint where the glass is thickest at the edges, and a dark ring where it meets the base. The nameplate is printed, not engraved, in a dense small sans. The base is black with a rim highlight, a sealing screw, and the lead seal on its red wire at the bottom left.

Left out: the GE monogram and the utility's stamped name plate; the potential and current coils and the register's gear train behind the plate; and the demand pointer of the I-70. The register advance is physical, not theatrical: at 4 rpm and Kh 7.2 the units pointer moves about ten degrees a minute, so the disc does the visible work — set a bigger watts or drag the bench slider to watch the dials move.
