THE SOURCE

Harvey Hubbell's pull-chain lampholder of 1896 put the switch inside the socket: a spring-loaded ratchet turned one notch by a beaded brass chain that leaves the shell through a little eyelet in its side, twelve millimetres below the cap. Bryant and Leviton built the same thing for the next seventy years, and it still hangs in cellars and on porches with a turned brass acorn on the end of the chain so a hand finds it in the dark.

The tells kept here: the spun brass shell with its cylinder highlight, rolled lip and a rim of paper insulator showing at the mouth; the stamped 250V 660W rating embossed on the shell; the eyelet in the side, not the bottom; a chain of small brass spheres on a fine link, each one catching its own highlight and the top ones brighter than the bottom; the slight S-curve the chain takes at rest under the fob's weight; the acorn's rounded nut, pointed tip and knurled band at the joint, in brass or in turned wood; the lamp base below with its first thread lines and a hint of glass. Pull the chain about twenty millimetres and it goes taut, the ratchet clicks, and the chain springs back in 150 ms and swings itself out over a second or so. The bulb warms up over 120 ms and cools over 250 ms, as an incandescent does.

Left out: the porcelain interior, the second chain some holders carry for a three-way lamp, and the cloth cord's full braid — only a short length of it enters the cap.
