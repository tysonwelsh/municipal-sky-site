THE SOURCE

Before anything was enclosed, a generating station's switchboard was a wall of white marble slabs with the conductors screwed to the face of it: bare copper knife switches, flat copper bus straps, brass terminal nuts, and a black composition handle to put your hand on. General Electric, Westinghouse and Cutler-Hammer all built them the same way between the 1890s and the 1930s, and the look — warm metal on cold stone — is the whole reason the things are still photographed.

The tells kept here: flat copper bars, not rods, pivoting in copper hinge blocks at the bottom and wedging into sprung jaws at the top; the jaws' slot visible when the switch is open and the leaves relaxed; the blades leaning out of the board toward you when open, foreshortened, with the handle bar in front and its shadow thrown across the marble; the turned black handle, thicker in the middle, on its copper cross-bar; big brass hex nuts with the bus strap and lug showing; two soft grey veins and a bevel on the slab's edge; and green-black verdigris where copper sits on copper at the hinges. The throw is heavy — 260 ms — and the jaws take the blade with a 2 px bite in the last tenth.

Left out: the arcing horns some station switches carried, the wall behind the board, and the barrier boards between poles. poles takes 1–3, board="slate" gives the black variant, and throw="double" adds a second set of jaws above, the value then stepping DOWN · OPEN · UP.
