THE SOURCE

The Roland TR-808's sixteen step keys are the most copied row of buttons in music: rectangular matte ABS caps on a 24 mm pitch, in four groups of four — red, orange, yellow, then a white that has gone ivory — each with its own small red LED in a separate hole above it, the step number silk-screened above that, and a thin orange rule running across the charcoal panel over the whole row.

The tells kept here: the four colours in group order; the LED above the key rather than in it, lighting when the step is on, instant up and with a 60 ms tail down, blooming into the panel around it; the cap as a plain radiused rectangle with a barely convex top, a soft central highlight, a 1 px darker rim and a hairline parting line; the 1 px black gap all round where the cap sits in its hole; the beat mark on steps 1, 5, 9 and 13; and the grime that yellows the corners of every cap — worst on the white ones, which were ivory when they left Roland and are worse now. Pressing travels 1.5 px in 40 ms onto a hard stop.

Left out: the group legends (1–4, 5–8 …) and the beat-counter that print between the keys, the sixteen-in-a-row count form — one element is one step — and the sequencer itself, though a run attribute will flash the LED in its turn at the given bpm so a host does not have to.
