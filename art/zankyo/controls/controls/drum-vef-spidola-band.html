THE SOURCE

The VEF Spidola (Riga, from 1960) is the transistor portable every Soviet flat had, and its signature is the band switch: not a knob but a hexagonal drum behind the dial window, with one printed scale strip glued to each flat. You turn the drum by its knurled rim, which pokes through the cream case at the right of the window, and the next band clicks into view while the old one tilts away at the top. A red hairline pointer rides along the strip and stays put when the band changes, because the tuning capacitor has not moved. The window sits in a chrome-plated pressed frame; below it the case runs into the chrome-edged speaker grille.

The tells kept here: six flat facets, each a real plane that foreshortens as it tilts (the numerals squash vertically, never curve) with the neighbouring strips peeking in at the top and bottom of the window; the band name in red at the left of every strip (ДВ, СВ, 49м, 41м, 31м, 25м) in a condensed Cyrillic grotesk; the pointer as a separate red hairline above the drum; the pressed chrome frame with its bright bevel; the knurled rim seen edge-on, its ribs placed by angle so they crowd toward the top and bottom of the slot and scroll as you turn it; a two-phase 60° click with a small detent bounce. Drag the strip sideways to tune (the pointer follows the finger); drag the rim up or down to roll the drum; the wheel tunes over the strip and changes band over the rim; the strip is a slider and the rim a spinbutton for the keyboard.

Left out: the drum's internal switch wafers and the tuning-shaft cord drive; the rest of the case with the telescopic aerial and the ВЭФ badge; the second row of markings (metres) most strips carried.
