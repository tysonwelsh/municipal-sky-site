THE SOURCE

The blasting machine is the box in every cartoon: quarter-sawn oak, varnished amber, brass straps screwed over the corners, two binding posts on the side for the firing line, a stamped brass plate, and a T-handle on a black steel rod standing a hundred millimetres above the lid. It is not a switch. Driving the handle down spins a small magneto through a rack and pinion, and the current it makes goes out to the caps — which is why the handle has to go all the way, and hard.

The tells kept here: the silhouette first — a wide turned handle high above a small box; oak with a drawn vertical grain and the pale medullary flecks of a quarter-sawn board, under a varnish that carries one broad sheen from the top left; brass corner straps with slotted screws set at their own angles; two binding posts with knurled nuts, the firing line wrapped round the lower one and dropping away; the stamped plate, its letters debossed so the bottom edge of each one catches light. The travel is the point: the drag maps non-linearly so the last centimetres come slowly, it fires only at the very bottom — a flash at the terminals, a fire event — and on release the rack lifts it back over 600 ms rather than snapping. The varnish is worn pale along the top edges and the ends of the handle are polished where the hands go.

Left out: the maker's name (the plate carries the type and the cap rating only), the leather strap, and the screw-down latch on the lid.
