THE SOURCE

In 1944 Hans Hilfiker, an engineer at the Swiss Federal Railways, drew a platform clock with no numerals at all: sixty black bars on a white ground, two black batons, and — added ten years later — a red second hand carrying a disc, the shape of a guard's signalling paddle. Every clock on the network is a slave of one master clock, and the way it keeps step is the thing everybody remembers. The second hand runs its lap in 58.5 seconds, stands still at the top, and waits for the master's minute pulse; when the pulse arrives the minute hand snaps forward one bar and the second hand sets off again. A train leaves on the jump, not on the sweep.

The tells kept here: no figures — sixty minute bars with twelve fatter hour bars, all rectangles ending on one clean ring; square-ended baton hands, the minute hand reaching exactly to the inner end of the minute bars; the red hand with its disc at the tip, about two hour-bars across, with a hot highlight where the light strikes it; the 58.5 s sweep, the 1.5 s pause dead at 12, and a minute jump that lands with a 0.3° bounce over 120 ms — the hour hand carries the same half-degree step; a dead-flat matte dial with only the faintest soft reflection across the flat glass; and a thin matte black rim with a hairline of light on its upper-left shoulder.

Left out: the SBB/CFF/FFS mark and the maker's name (the dial carries neither on most platform clocks anyway), the double-sided bracket and its arm, and the second hand's real electrical behaviour — here it is a free-running motor synchronised to the host's clock rather than a slave waiting on a wire.
