THE SOURCE

The National PW-D is the dial on the HRO, the receiver every 1930s and wartime listening post was built around: a hand-sized black phenolic knob with ten finger scallops around a thick chamfered rim, driving the gang capacitor through a precision worm. Ten turns take it end to end, and the reading is not printed around the knob but inside it — a numbered disc turns behind a small window at twelve o'clock, framed by a bright nickel escutcheon with an engraved 0–10 vernier along its lower edge.

The tells kept here: the ten scallops cut through the rim, each a lit dimple with a shadowed top-left wall; the chamfer as one crisp ring of highlight above a dead-matte face; the window's nickel escutcheon, the numeral disc drawn as a cylinder seen edge-on so the figures foreshorten and crowd as they reach the window's edges; the vernier lines reading 0–10 against the disc's divisions, half a division by eye; a small turn counter under the window that climbs a digit per turn; an engraved word plate on the panel below. The drag follows the finger's angle and the dial coasts on release, slowing over about 600 ms; it stops softly at 0 and 500.

Left out: the worm-drive backlash you can feel on a real one, and the bandspread calibration charts that lived in the lid.
