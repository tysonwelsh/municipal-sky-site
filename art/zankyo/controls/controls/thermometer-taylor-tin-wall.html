THE SOURCE

Taylor Instrument of Rochester, New York made the wall thermometer that hung by the back door of every feed store, garage and soda cooler in America: a strip of steel dipped in white porcelain enamel, rolled at the edges, two holes for the nails, a glass tube of red spirit held on by two brass spring clips, and the Fahrenheit scale printed down one side of it with the Celsius down the other. The advertiser paid for the block at the top and the slogan at the bottom.

The tells kept here: the thin red column with its clearly rounded meniscus and the fat bulb below it, the long white highlight down the glass and the grey shadow on its right; ticks every two degrees and a numeral every ten, Fahrenheit left and Celsius right, printed straight onto the enamel; two brass clips, polished on their crowns and dark in the crease; the chips — one at a top corner, one at the bottom, and a ring of them round each nail hole — showing rust and bare steel, because this hung outside for forty winters; the blue-grey shading where the enamel rolls over the edge and one soft gloss reflection across the top left. The spirit is slow: it eases to a new reading over about a second and a half and the meniscus keeps its shape all the way.

Left out: the advertiser's own artwork (a generic wordmark stands in), the paper tag on the back, and the second decimal scale some models carried for canning.
