THE SOURCE

The Hammond drawbar has not changed since the Model A of 1935: nine bars per manual, each a black steel stem with a moulded phenolic cap, sliding in a chrome escutcheon over the walnut console. Pulling a bar toward you adds that harmonic in eight steps, and the stem reveals a white-boxed numeral for each step as it comes out, so the registration can be read from the bench — 888 000 000 is the classic jazz setting. The caps are colour-coded: brown for the sub-fundamental and sub-third, white for the octaves, black for the odd harmonics, reading brown-brown-white-white-black-white-black-black-white across the set.

The tells kept here: the scalloped front edge of each cap — four concave bites — and the cap's thickness, drawn as a solid block with its side and its bottom showing; the numerals 1–8 in white boxes on the black stem, emerging one at a time from under the chrome so the number just below the rail is the current step; the footage engraved and black-filled on the chrome beneath each channel, with real fraction characters; the caps sitting in a shallow black channel with the stem's shadow falling into it; the white caps yellowed and the 8′ cap worn glossy where the fingers go; the brown caps with a satin sheen. The bar is pulled down the screen to increase, with a soft detent every step; wheel down pulls one step; arrows step, Shift-arrows run to the end; Home pushes fully in.

Left out: the preset-key logic and the second manual's set; the console lip is a strip of walnut rather than the whole fallboard.
