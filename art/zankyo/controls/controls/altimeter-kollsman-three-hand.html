THE SOURCE

The Kollsman sensitive altimeter — the C-12 and the AN-5760 pattern that filled American and British cockpits from the war onward — is a three-and-an-eighth-inch instrument in a black crackle case: ten numerals round the dial with nought at the top, three hands on one boss reading hundreds, thousands and ten-thousands of feet, a barometric window at three o'clock showing inches of mercury, a smaller one at nine in millibars, a knurled setting knob at seven, and a striped flag that shows in a little window while you are below ten thousand feet and slides away as you climb through it.

The tells kept here: the hands are geared exactly 1 : 10 : 100 — the long thin hundreds hand with its lume tip turns once per thousand feet, the short broad spade with the black centre stripe once per ten thousand, and the hairline with the little white triangle rides the rim once per hundred thousand; long lume bars sit halfway between the numerals with a faint green halo; the Kollsman window is a recess with a highlight along its top edge and a hairline index; the knurl on the knob is drawn by angle so it crowds at the edges; the case is black crackle — a fine random speckle — with a bevel highlight on the bezel's upper left and four tiny screws at the corners of the square mounting flange behind the round bezel; the arc of "CALIBRATED TO 20,000 FEET" sits inside the bottom of the dial.

Left out: the rear static connection and the instrument's serial plate; the barometric drums do not roll as the knob turns (the knob is decoration — this is an output, not an input); the hundreds hand's quiver decays after a couple of seconds instead of running forever, so a gallery of these is cheap.
