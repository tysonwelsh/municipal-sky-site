THE SOURCE

A Stanley No. 30-series level is a rosewood beam bound in brass with a cast-brass port let into its face, and inside the port a barrel-ground glass vial of green-yellow spirit with a bubble in it. Two black lines are etched around the glass; when the bubble sits squarely between them the work is level, and a carpenter will tap the beam and watch the bubble swim back and forth before believing it.

The tells kept here: the vial is barrel-ground, so the bore rises toward the middle and the bubble is biggest and brightest at the centre of its travel, squashing and dimming as it runs to the ends; it rides above the fluid's centre line, hugging the top of the glass, with a white crescent at its top-left, a dark green rim, and a soft shadow dropped on the fluid beneath it. The two etched lines read as short vertical ellipses and thicken where the bubble's curvature refracts them. The brass port's opening is chamfered — bright along its top edge, dark along its lower one — with a dark inner shadow on the glass, two flat-head screws whose slots sit at whatever angle they stopped at, and a line of grime in the joint against the wood. The maker's line is engraved, not printed. The bubble is light and the spirit is viscous: it overshoots about a tenth, wobbles twice, and never quite stops trembling. Driven past ±1 it pins against the end of the vial, flattened. body="aluminium" swaps the rosewood for a grained alloy beam.

Left out: the level's other two vials (plumb and 45°), the brass top plate with its oval maker's stamp, and the dozen dents a century of site work leaves in the binding.
