THE SOURCE

The Yamaha CS-80 (1977) carries a 350 mm resistive ribbon above the left end of its keyboard: a matte black strip lying flush in the black panel inside a slim silver-anodised frame with a countersunk screw at each end. There is no scale on it, no centre mark, no detent — you slide a finger along it and the pitch goes with the finger; lift off and it returns to nothing.

The tells kept here: the strip is dead matte, with a faint weave in the surface and a lighter polished band down the middle third where a thousand slides have worn it; the hairline dark gap between ribbon and frame, with a glint along the frame's inner edge where the light from above-left catches it; the anodised frame's fine grain running the length of the strip under one broad soft highlight; the two countersunk screws; one line of white silk-screen beneath, which is the only printing anywhere near it. Nothing shows the value but the fingertip's own soft shadow — released, it springs to zero in 120 ms.

Left out: the CS-80 itself — the keyboard, the wooden end cheeks and the brass rail beside it — and the brand, which on the real panel sits far to the left of the ribbon.
