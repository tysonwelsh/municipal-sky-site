THE SOURCE

Every school physics laboratory in Britain between the wars had one of these on the bench: a glazed porcelain tube wound end to end with a tight helix of dull nichrome, carried on two black cast-iron end frames, with a brass bar above it and a brass shoe sliding on the bar and pressing on the winding. Three terminals with knurled nuts — two ends of the winding, one on the bar — let it be wired as a rheostat or as a potential divider, and a typed paper label gave its ohms and its current rating.

The tells kept here: the winding runs the whole length of the tube as a fine repeating pattern of turns, compressed into the cylinder's shading at the crest and the underside, with a pale worn track along the top where the shoe has scraped the oxide off; a glimpse of the bare glazed porcelain at each end; the brass bar polished bright only along the stretch the slider has travelled; the spring finger reaching down from the slider block to touch the wire; a black Bakelite knob on top; three brass terminals with knurled nuts; chipped black paint on the frames' edges; and the typed label, glued to the left frame and lifting at its corner.

Left out: the individual turns are a repeating gradient rather than a hundred and fifty drawn wires — at this size they are a pixel and a half apart and a drawn turn would alias into mush. The rear terminal posts and the bench clamp are not shown.
