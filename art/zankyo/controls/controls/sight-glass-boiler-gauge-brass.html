THE SOURCE

Every boiler that ever made steam carried one of these on its backhead: a tube of thick glass held between two brass cocks, with a drain cock under the bottom one and a guard in front in case the glass let go. The fireman's whole job hangs on it, and it is read from six feet away in a shaking cab, so the backplate behind the glass is painted with steep black diagonals — where there is water the glass acts as a lens and the stripes appear to lean the other way, and the level is the hard line where they reverse.

The tells kept here: the striped backplate and the reversal at the water line, with the stripes in the water also a touch coarser because the glass magnifies them; the meniscus as a bright curve with a dark line beneath it; the tube's white highlight down its left and the grey-green shadow down its right, over a cylinder gradient so it reads as a tube and not a slot; the two big brass cocks with their turned bodies, packing nuts and long lever handles both laid over the same way, which is how you know the gauge is open; the brass polished bright on the handle crowns where hands go and dull with verdigris in the threads and under the nuts; the drain cock and its little spout; the red lowest-safe-water line across the plate; and the engraved brass plate at the foot. With boil the level surges about half a hertz and bubbles climb the glass.

Left out: the wire guard's mesh (the frame is here, the mesh would fill the window at this size), the union threads into the backhead, and the maker's name (a generic engraving stands in).
