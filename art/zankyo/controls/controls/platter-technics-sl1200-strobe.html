THE SOURCE

On a Technics SL-1200 MK2 the pitch is checked with a stroboscope built out of nothing but casting and a lamp: four rows of dots are cast into the platter's rim and painted around, and a red-orange neon in the little black housing at the front-left flashes at twice mains — 100 Hz in Europe, 120 Hz in Japan and America. Each row has the number of dots that makes it come out even at one speed and one mains frequency: 216, 180, 160 and 133. The row that matches stands still. The others crawl.

The tells kept here: the four rows are real dot counts and the drift is the real arithmetic — apparent rate = rotation − (nearest whole number of flashes per dot) × flash rate ÷ dots — so the matched row freezes at 0 %, creeps forward or back in proportion to the pitch error, and the other three run at their own speeds and directions; the 45/50 Hz row's 133 dots are a hair off exact, so it creeps even at zero, as it does on the real deck. The rim highlight is fixed on the spun aluminium while the dots travel under it; the rubber mat's ribbed edge sits above the rim; the neon flickers two per cent at the mains rate and spills warm orange onto the rim in front of its window; the direct drive gets up to speed in 0.7 s and coasts down over two.

Left out: the tone arm, the pitch fader and the start button, the 33/45 buttons and the target light — this is the front-left quadrant only. The word TECHNICS is not printed anywhere.
