THE SOURCE

A Dekatron is a cold-cathode counting tube: a ring of thirty fine wire cathodes standing off a mica disc inside a one-inch glass envelope, a disc anode in the middle, and a neon fill. A glow discharge sits on one of the ten main cathodes; a pulse on the guide electrodes walks it round to the next one, two guide cathodes at a time, and the tube remembers its count with no memory at all. The Ericsson GS10C was the British one — Beeston, 1949 — and 828 of them are the store and the registers of the Harwell Dekatron, which is still running at Bletchley.

The tells kept here: seen end-on you read the thirty pins as short radial dashes — ten long main ones, twenty shorter guides between — around the dull grey anode disc, with the mica sheet faintly behind them. One cathode carries an orange dot with a pale core and a soft halo, and the two guides either side of it pick up a faint glow of their own. A step hops in three eight-millisecond sub-steps, main → guide → guide → main, so a fast count reads as a rotating smear with a trail of about sixty degrees; a negative pulse hops the other way. The glass face is domed: a broad crescent reflection at the upper left, a bright rim ring, and the whole thing sits in a black bakelite holder with a keyway slot and a printed 0–9 escutcheon, zero at the top. Purple mode is the Soviet ОГ-4's tint.

Left out: the eleven-pin base and the wiring behind it, the "double-pulse" GC10B guide arrangement, and the way a real tube's glow fattens as the anode current rises.
