THE SOURCE

The RCA 6E5 is the little tube that turned tuning a radio into a game. Its end is a shallow cone of willemite phosphor that glows green under an electron beam, with a dark wedge cut out of it by the target's control vane. Tune off station and the wedge yawns open about 90°; tune in and it closes to a hairline. Its cousin the 6U5 is driven harder and the two edges cross, throwing a brighter sliver where they overlap.

The tells kept here: the light is a ring, not a disc — the cathode shield cap sits black in the middle with a faint orange filament glint along its lower edge, and the phosphor is an annulus between the cap and the glass, hotter on its inner edge and darker at its rim. The shadow is a wedge opening downward toward 6 o'clock, its edges softened by about two pixels of feather and lifted a little brighter just outside the cut. The phosphor carries a fine grain, and the glow blooms a few pixels past the ring and washes green onto the chrome escutcheon it is set into. The escutcheon is a narrow chrome ring with a hard horizon and a bright top-left highlight, let into walnut veneer, casting a dark inner shadow onto the glass. Nothing about a hot tube is ever quite steady: the glow breathes about three per cent over four seconds, and a large jump in signal makes it flicker for a frame.

Left out: the tube's envelope, base and pins (you only ever see the end through the hole), the triode section's contribution, and the burned-in dark ring that an old 6E5 acquires after a few thousand hours on one strong station.
