THE SOURCE

The Toledo Scale Company built its trade on a pendulum-and-cam mechanism instead of a spring, and advertised it with the phrase painted on the dial itself: no springs — honest weight. A spring creeps and ages; a pendulum weighs the same in 1921 and 1951. The counter model's dial is a fan — a 170° sector of white porcelain enamel standing on a squat cast-iron frame, the pointer pivoting at the bottom centre so the whole scale can sit low on a grocer's counter under the customer's eye.

The tells kept here: the fan sector with the pivot at the bottom and the near-horizontal side edges; pounds in large black slab-serif figures with a comb of subdivisions between them and a red tick at every whole pound; the narrow chrome bezel that follows the arc and returns down both sides; the porcelain's soft above-left sheen and its crazing; the frame's cream enamel chipped through to dark iron at the shoulder and at the plinth's corner; the slogan in two arcs above the pivot with the capacity line beneath; the long tapered black blade with its round counterweight swinging in a recess cut into the casting; and a hint of the brass pan below. The pointer swings past the weight and comes back — three decaying swings over about nine-tenths of a second, then dead still, because there is no spring to tremble.

Left out: the Toledo shield and script logo (the phrase is generic, the logo is not), the rear column and the pan's full body, and — at 30 lb capacity — the true sixteen-per-pound ounce comb, whose ticks would fall 0.8 px apart at this size; the comb steps down to quarter-pounds and only returns to full ounces on the 10 lb dial, where there is room for them.
