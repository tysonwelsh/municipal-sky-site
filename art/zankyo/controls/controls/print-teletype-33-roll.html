THE SOURCE

The Teletype Model 33 was the terminal of the first timesharing decade: a typebox on a carriage, a rubber platen, an inked ribbon and a roll of cheap yellow-white paper, printing uppercase only at ten characters a second behind a grey-green painted guide bar with a serrated tear edge. Every character is a separate hammer blow, so no two strikes on a real roll are quite the same weight or quite on the baseline.

The tells kept here: per-character jitter — ±0.4 px of baseline, ±0.5° of rotation and a spread of ink weights with the odd faint strike; the whole paper jolting 1 px as each hammer lands; the 100 ms line advance with a double-strike ghost on the last character of a line; the correction typed as XXXXX struck over the mistake by backspacing the carriage (the machine cannot erase); the BELL at the end of the session as an overprinted dot; the paper's fibre grain, its ragged roll-cut right margin and the shadow where it curls forward out of the slot; the platen with its knob, and the ribbon fading the whole page toward grey as ribbon drops.

Left out: the machine around the paper — the keyboard, the paper-tape punch and reader, the call control unit. The roll is drawn at columns="52" so the type is legible at card size; the element's default is the machine's real 72-column line, which renders the same page in 7 px type.
