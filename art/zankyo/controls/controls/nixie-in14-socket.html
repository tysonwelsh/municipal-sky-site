THE SOURCE

The ИН-14 is the common Soviet side-view Nixie: a 19 mm glass envelope with a domed top and an evacuation tip, ten thin nickel cathodes shaped like numerals stacked one behind the other, a fine hexagonal anode mesh in front of them, and thirteen pins into a black phenolic socket. Strike one cathode at about 170 V and the neon-argon fill wraps it in an orange glow that is closer to a flame than to a lamp.

The tells kept here: the numerals are the tube's own single-stroke cathode shapes drawn as SVG paths — the 5 is the 2 turned upside down, the 3 has a flat top, the 4 is open at the top, the 1 is a plain bar. The nine unlit cathodes stay visible as a dark tangle, each offset a fraction of a pixel from the one behind it, and the ones standing in front of the lit digit cut across its glow. The glow is built in four layers — a wide soft cast, a 6 px halo, the body of the stroke, and a hot white-orange core — and flickers about one percent at 30 Hz so it breathes. Over everything inside the front face is the hexagonal anode mesh. A digit change cross-fades over about 120 ms and the outgoing cathode dies away through dim orange. The glow spills onto the phenolic socket and the board below it.

Left out: the RF getter flash on the inside of the glass, the pin numbering on the socket skirt, and the slight blue haze that a real tube gets on the mesh when it is run hard.
