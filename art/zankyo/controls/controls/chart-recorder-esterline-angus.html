THE SOURCE

The Esterline-Angus Model AW is the black crackle box with the chrome door that sat on every test floor from the 1930s on: a roll of 4-inch chart paper crawls downward past a capillary pen fed from a little brass well, and the pen writes in red on a green-printed grid. Because the pen swings on an arm, its path is an arc — so the chart's time lines are printed as arcs struck from the pen's pivot, and a moment in time is a curve, not a straight rule across the paper. That is what "curvilinear chart" means, and it is the difference between this machine and the linkage-driven rectilinear recorders.

The tells kept here: the curvilinear grid, drawn with the same radius and centre as the pen arm, so the arcs and the pen's path are the same curve; the paper running down with the pen writing near the top; the red ink laid down thicker where the pen dwells and thinner on a fast excursion, with a small blot where it stopped, and a faint bleed into the paper either side of the line; the sprocket perforations down one edge; the heavy grid line every fourth division and the printed 0–100 scale and hour stamp that come round with the paper; the black wrinkle paint with the light from above-left, the chrome door frame with its hard horizon, one broad diagonal reflection in the glass that is not the shape of anything behind it, and the brass ink well below.

Left out: the clock drive and the take-up spool behind the paper, the range switch, the door latch and hinge pin, and the maker's name — the plate reads generically.
