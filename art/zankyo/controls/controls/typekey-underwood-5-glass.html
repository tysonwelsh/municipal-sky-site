THE SOURCE

The Underwood No. 5 — the machine that fixed the shape of the typewriter from 1900 until the 1930s — has four banks of round keys, each a disc of glass over a black paper legend, held in a nickel-plated brass ring with a rolled bead, on a long japanned steel stem that rises out of the frame. Nickel is warmer and yellower than chrome, and the plating on the front edge of a much-used key wears through to the brass underneath.

The tells kept here: the rolled bead read as three concentric lines — bright outer edge, dark groove, bright inner edge; the glass sitting about a millimetre below the ring, so the ring throws a hairline crescent shadow across the top-left of the glass and a band of reflected light glows along the bottom-right; one wide, soft, slightly curved reflection over the top-left third with a faint blue-grey cast; cream-white letters (never pure white) on warm matte black paper; the brass showing at the front edge and a grey scuff over the legend of the most-used keys; the stem rising from behind with the frame's gold pinstripe either side of it. Travel is long — eight pixels of it — with a hard clack at the bottom that darkens the stem for a frame, and a slower rise back. The three keys carry different stem lengths, which is how the banks stagger up and back.

Left out: the type-bar and its linkage, the ribbon-colour levers, the decal on the frame, and the ring's true torus — this is a flat-on elevation, so the bead is drawn with light rather than geometry.
