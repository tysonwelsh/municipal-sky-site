THE SOURCE

Between about 1850 and 1920 an English microscope stand — Ross, Powell & Lealand, Beck, or a Leitz from Wetzlar — focused by a rack and pinion: a strip of teeth cut diagonally across the face of the limb, a pinion in a cast housing, and a pair of knurled brass heads on a common axle so either hand could drive it. The whole stand was lacquered brass on a japanned horseshoe foot, and a fine-focus micrometer with a silvered divided drum stood beside the limb for the last half millimetre.

The tells kept here: the rack's teeth are cut on the diagonal, bright on the top land and dark in the root; the lacquer is duller and more orange on the flat faces and worn through to bright brass on the knobs and on the front of the rack, where hands and the pinion have been. The knurl is a straight knurl of 44 ridges projected around the rim, so it crowds at the top and bottom of each knob, and it scrolls one full turn per 6 mm of climb. A window in the housing shows the pinion's teeth meshing with the rack. The draw-tube, eyepiece and slide collar ride up together; the micrometer's silvered drum, an engraved LONDON, a serial number, chipped japan on the horseshoe foot and a ring of old dust at its base finish it.

Left out: the sub-stage mirror and condenser, the objective nosepiece, the stage plate and its clips — this is the limb alone, which is the control. The fine attribute shifts the tube ±0.5 mm without turning the coarse knobs, as the micrometer screw would; the drag is geared down to a deliberate 250 px for the full 30 mm rather than the rack's true pitch.
