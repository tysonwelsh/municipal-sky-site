THE SOURCE

On a National Cash Register brass machine of the 1890s — the Model 79, the 313, the ornate 442 out of Dayton — the amount keys are round porcelain or celluloid discs held up on tall nickel-plated stems, arranged in columns by denomination, standing out of a black-japanned key frame above a cast-brass case covered in scrollwork. You did not tap these keys; you drove them down an inch and the machine answered with a bell.

The tells kept here: the mushroom-on-a-stalk silhouette — the stem is far longer than the key is wide; cold glaze, a sharp white highlight with a blue-grey cast in the shadow, which is what tells porcelain from plastic; the thin bright nickel rim showing the disc's thickness under it, worn through to brass at the lower right where a thumb has been for thirty years; the stem as a bright cylinder with a dark core line down it; the staggered column heights; and the brass case below with its scroll in low relief. The 5¢ is crazed — fine grey hairlines in the glaze. Travel is long and mechanical: a 12 px drop in 70 ms, a hard stop, 160 ms back up, and the drawer bell implied by one frame of light across the brass.

Left out: the rest of the column (five keys and their frame), the indicator flags and the glass window above, the cast dolphins and the maker's cartouche — the scroll here is suggested, not a portrait of any one machine's casting.
